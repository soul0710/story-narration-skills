# Characters, narrator voice, and dialogue

Sources: jtydhr88 `sw-character-conflict` and `sw-dialogue` (McKee *Dialogue*, Walter, Hicks, Egri,
Snyder, Lu Jun), story-skills `character-management` (voice-words / voice-avoid), oh-story
`dialogue-mastery.md` (power games, agenda). **[src]** from source, **[sn]** sn heuristic.

## 1. Character sheet (bible/characters.md + ledger.json)

For each principal character:
- `id` (ascii kebab-case: `lan`, `ba-noi`), display name, age, role (protagonist / opponent / ally /
  catalyst / mirror / mentor).
- **Visual signature** in words the shots can repeat verbatim (age, hair, clothing, one object) [sn,
  after drama-skills continuity lock].
- **Want** (external, conscious, observable) vs **need** (internal) — the need matters only when it makes
  the character act differently [src: long-video-scriptwriting fiction.md, Hicks].
- **Fears, beliefs, limits** — what they will not do, and what pressure would make them do it anyway
  (prevents personality bent to serve the plot).
- Flaw / lie believed; ghost (past wound) — revealed late, through action; **backstory is a secret kept
  for the moment it turns a scene** (McKee: "using backstory for a turn is the most powerful exposition").
- Public identity vs true identity (drives reveals).
- **Voice** [src]: directness (says it straight / circles / deflects), what they avoid talking about, 3–5 `voice-words` (vocabulary from their life and trade — nouns and verbs show their
  world, modifiers show personality), 2–3 `voice-avoid`, sentence length, register, dialect flavour
  (Bắc/Trung/Nam), **xưng hô** with every other principal [sn — in Vietnamese, a change in xưng hô
  (tôi→em, anh→mày, con→tôi) is a turning point; use it deliberately].
- Arc: start state → turning points (chapter ids) → end state. Each claimed change needs a pressure test,
  a choice, a cost, and a visibly changed strategy [src: drama-skills STY-12].
- Payoff function (who humiliates, who witnesses, who reveals).

Orchestration (Egri): characters differ on the central value; put opposites in the same scene.
Principal count [sn]: ≤5 for 30–60 min, ≤7 for 90 min+. Merge characters that share a function.

## 2. The narrator [sn, built on sw-dialogue "narrative vs dramatic dialogue"]

Decide once, in `bible/narrator.md`:
- **Person**: third-person storyteller (default), first-person protagonist, or first-person witness
  (a grandchild telling the grandmother's story — allows foreshadowing and emotional distance).
- **Distance**: close (inside one head) or wide (village storyteller).
- **Tone**: warm / wry / ominous / restrained. Restraint lands harder than adjectives.
- **Signature devices** (1–2): recurring phrase, "Về sau…" foreshadowing, addressing the listener.
- Direct narration has two legitimate powers (McKee): **speed** (compressing years in a sentence) and
  **counterpoint** (the narrator's irony against the scene). Anything else — show it.
- **Forbidden**: stating the moral, summarising what the scene already showed, over-foreshadowing.

## 3. Dialogue is action [src: McKee]

Every line performs an inner action. Before writing a line: *What does the character want now? What
action gets it? What words perform that action?* Label the subtext with a verb ("xoa dịu", "dồn ép",
"né tránh"). Three layers: said / unsaid (conscious concealment) / unsayable (unconscious need, surfaces
only under pressure). If a line implies nothing beneath its surface, enrich it or cut it.

**Agenda** [src: oh-story]: each character enters a conversation wanting something from it; the
collision of agendas is the tension. Same agenda = recitation. Tone = relationship × occasion × purpose.

## 4. Exposition as ammunition [src]

- Release only the minimum, never early; repeat the important fact.
- Characters reveal information only when it wins them something: "Mày vẫn y như hai mươi năm trước ở
  trường làng, cái thằng hôi rình ấy" tells us "twenty years", "school" without anyone reciting it.
- **Never let a character tell another what both already know** (Walter #28).
- Put necessary explanation into an unexpected, charged setting.
- Legitimate on-the-nose lines: "Rốt cuộc mày muốn gì?" and "Con có sao không?" — and non-realistic genres.

## 5. Four families of flaws [src: McKee]

- **Credibility**: empty words (telling what's known), sentimentality (words beyond the feeling),
  over-awareness (characters analysing themselves like a therapist), excuses mistaken for motive.
  Melodrama is not over-expression — it is under-motivation.
- **Language**: clichés (write past your first choice), character-neutral language, ornate lines that
  shout "look how clever", flat language. Prefer concrete, familiar, short, direct, active, expressive.
- **Content**: on-the-nose subtext ("Anh yêu em, anh yêu em" at candlelight — unplayable); interior
  monologue as the author's megaphone.
- **Design**: repeated beats (same tactic three times — the third cry becomes comedy); the key word not at
  the end of the line; turning points too early/late/absent.

## 6. Techniques [src]

- **Suspense sentence**: hold the core word to the end ("Vậy sao hôm đó cô lại đưa cho tôi… con dao?").
  Mix with cumulative and balanced sentences so none becomes wallpaper.
- **Economy**: "the writer isn't paid by the word". Each line: does it advance plot, tell something new,
  or could it be shorter?
- **Argument**: in art, no two people agree; let every line challenge the next.
- **Pauses and silence**: tighten before the turn, breathe after it; silence is the most economical
  language — a single breath can be the turning point.
- **Trialogue**: a stiff two-hander improves when both fight through a third thing (an object, a person,
  a memory, a bottle).
- **Power games** [src: oh-story]: line length = status. The one in control speaks short and calm; the one
  under pressure speaks long and emotional. Patterns: *suppression* (a 3–5 line tirade → one word),
  *reversal* (arrogance → one line of fact → silence), *heart gone dead* (replies shrink: explanation →
  "Ừ." → "Tùy.").

## 7. Character-specific speech [src]

"The more complex the psychology, the more distinct the dialogue." Vocabulary betrays character. Cover-
the-names test: hide the names — can you tell who speaks? Swap test (Wei Minglun): give the line to
another character; if it still works, it's a stock speech, not this person's words.

## 8. Dialogue in narrated video [sn]

- The ear can't see quotation marks: attribute clearly (tag + narration verb); keep exchanges 2–6 lines.
- Let the narrator carry connective tissue; give dialogue to turns, reveals and confrontations.
- Dialogue share around 15–35% of spoken syllables is a **starting point, not a rule** — a lone-survivor
  story may have almost none; a courtroom story much more.

## 9. Dialogue checklist [src: sw-dialogue §9, condensed]

1. The action beneath each line (a verb)? If none, cut or rewrite.
2. Cover-the-names test passes? Vocabulary shows their world?
3. Anyone telling someone what both know? Is it ammunition? If not, cut.
4. On-the-nose? (Exceptions above.)
5. Stiff two-hander → find the third thing.
6. Repeated beats?
7. Core word at the end of the line?
8. Filler ("Anh biết đấy", "Nghe này", "Ừm", "À"), exclamation marks, bracketed acting directions stacked?
9. Speech longer than 4 lines: does every line add new information?
10. Two people agreeing anywhere? (Agreement is boring.)
11. Could this beat be a silence or an action instead?
12. Is melodrama coming from missing motivation?
