# Scene craft: designing a scene that turns

Sources: jtydhr88 `sw-scene-craft` (McKee, Field, Hicks, Walter, Hauge, Lu Jun), drama-skills
`short-drama-write/script-craft.md` (scene engine card), story-skills `scene-craft/scene-sequel.md` and
`chapter-writing` (scene outcomes), oh-story `writing-craft.md`. **[src]** from source, **[sn]** heuristic.

## 1. What a scene is [src]

Continuous action in (relatively) continuous time/place, through conflict, that **turns a charged value**
on at least one level. Record the value at the start and at the end; if equal, ask why the scene exists —
it is almost always exposition. Cut it and weave the information into a scene that turns.
Every scene must do two things at once (Hicks): push the plot with new information, and reveal more of
the protagonist.

## 2. Scene engine card — fill before writing a conflict scene [src: drama-skills]

```
Necessity:   what choice / evidence / relationship change is lost if this scene is cut?
Entry state: what facts, action and pressure do the characters walk in with?
Focal goal:  what does the focal character want someone to change *in this scene*? (actable, not "be respected")
Opposition:  what does the other side want, and with what leverage do they block it?
Arena:       how do space, task or object force the conflict into action (not sitting and talking)?
Strategy:    what do they try first — how do they change tactics when blocked?
Turn:        which fact, power shift, or decision makes the original plan fail?
Exit state:  who gained/lost what; why must the next scene happen now?
```

Opposition is not "no": it redefines the problem, trades conditions, threatens, reveals, delays,
exploits the relationship. Goals must be actable ("khiến bà thừa nhận bức thư là của ông, trước mặt cả
nhà"), and may fail, but the attempt must be performable. Choice and consequence drive major turns;
coincidence may open a problem but never solves the core dilemma. Don't invent precision (records,
procedures, numbers, mechanisms) the story hasn't established just to display a rule.

## 3. McKee's five steps [src]

1. Define the conflict: who drives, what they want; opposition directly opposed, not tangential.
2. Note the opening value (+/−).
3. Break into beats — action/reaction units; label subtext with verbs ("dỗ dành", "gạt phắt đi"). A new
   beat needs a clear change of behaviour.
4. Note the closing value and compare.
5. Find the turning point — created only by **action or revelation**; effect: surprise → curiosity →
   insight → new direction.
Earliest symptom of a bad scene: **repeated beats** (same tactic, different words).

## 4. Scene outcomes and sequels [src: story-skills]

Tag each goal-driven scene (and each chapter in `project.json.outcome`):
`yes` (got it — use sparingly), `no` (failed), `yes-but` (got it, with a new cost/complication),
`no-and` (failed and it got worse). Three `yes` in a row = no pressure (sn.py warns).

After a setback, consider a **sequel** (Swain): **reaction** (one physical, involuntary beat — not a page
of wallowing) → **dilemma** (two bad options with explicit stakes) → **decision** (theirs, and it costs
something) → the action that opens the next scene. Skip it after a clear victory or when the next scene
opens mid-action. Four or more scenes without a sequel → no breath.

For narrated video the sequel is where the narrator earns intimacy: a short reflective passage *anchored
in a concrete image*, then the decision.

## 5. Entry, exit, rhythm [src]

- **Enter late, leave early**; leave before the conflict is fully resolved so the tension carries.
- Transitions: find the "third element" shared or contrasted between two scenes — a gesture, object,
  line, quality of light, sound, idea. In narration, a spoken bridge line can do it.
- Speed up toward act climaxes by shortening scenes — to *earn* the pause; the big reversal scene itself
  can be long and slow.
- Between two heavy scenes, a short low-conflict scene separates them and lets consequences land.

## 6. Location choice [src: Walter, Lu Jun]

Ask "why here, why now?". For key scenes list a dozen possible locations and choose by three criteria:
serves the idea, exposes the character, makes the conflict harder. Avoid static defaults (sitting in a
living room, a café, a car, an office) unless the place becomes the centre of the action. Respect the
location budget in generability.md.

## 7. Action over talk; details; props [src]

- Any action beats no action; action woven into the story beats all. First ask: can this scene be told
  purely visually (+ narration)?
- Replace states with actions: "cô rất giận" → she tears up the invitation.
- Details must be true, specific, visible, and carry the era. Concept vs. detail: "anh ta sĩ diện" vs.
  "anh ta ngồi trước cổng xỉa răng bằng que diêm, cho cả xóm thấy mình vừa ăn thịt".
- Don't show off a detail: a repetition is justified only if it advances plot, conflict, or character.
- Props do work: structural core, conflict accelerator, character, symbol, theme window. **Prop chain**
  (The Peach Blossom Fan): each change of the prop's meaning is caused by *another person's action* and
  lands on a value turn. See mechanism-and-agency.md (rule of three).
- Numbers are narrative tools [src: oh-story]: specific figures carry weight ("tám năm", "tám mươi triệu");
  escalate them; re-anchor every new number to something the audience already knows the value of
  ("một triệu hai — đủ tiền học một kỳ của em gái"). Spell them out in narration.

## 8. Suspense: give the audience the key [src: Lu Jun, Archer]

Surprise is "one night's treat"; foreknowledge lasts. Often tell the audience what the character doesn't
know (the poison is in the bowl) and let them dread. Delay before release: crouch before the jump, build
in three layers. The best suspense is suspense of **character and fate**, not only of information.

## 9. Scene checklist [src]

1. Why here, now? Would another place make the conflict harder?
2. Value at stake; open +/−, close +/−. Unchanged → cut or merge.
3. Each character's want, tactic, hidden want. Opposition direct?
4. Beat list: repeated beats? Each beat closer to the turn? Turn by action or revelation, not early/late?
5. Entered late, left early?
6. "Nothing happens" stretches (small talk, ordering food)? Characters stuck in states instead of actions?
7. Could it work without dialogue? Which small action could be magnified?
8. Suspense: what key is given to the audience? Enough delay?
9. Details true, specific, visible, not showing off? Prop doing which job?
10. Generable (generability.md)?
